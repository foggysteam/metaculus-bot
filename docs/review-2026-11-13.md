## Massachusetts Question 8 (repeal of recreational marijuana), -14.1

- Link: https://www.metaculus.com/questions/46198
- **Result:** Yes got 24%.
- **Our forecast (24 Oct):** median 30.5, 80% range 23.6 to 38.0.
  - All 5 forecasts agreed closely, with medians from 29.75 to 30.75.
  - The question closed on 25 Oct, so there was no chance to update.

**What went wrong.** The research had what was needed:
- All five polls.
- The note "Likely-voter polls show lower Yes numbers than registered-voter polls".
- The note that undecided voters on ballot measures tend to break toward No.

Two things then pulled the forecasts too high:

1. **The research's summary figure.** It said "about 30% on average" for the Yes share, with undecided voters removed. That number weights the two registered-voter polls (two-way Yes about 34% and 38%) the same as the three likely-voter polls. The likely-voter polls give about 31%, 25% and 23%, an average of about 26%. All five forecasters started from 30.
2. **An upward adjustment against the evidence.** The forecaster quoted below talked itself up from the polls, citing a "structural floor":
   - Republicans back repeal at 44%.
   - 46% voted against legalisation in 2016.
   - Other losing Massachusetts measures got 36% to 43%.

   It also said the trend and the likely-voter polls pointed to "the mid-to-high 20s". It then set its median at 30.5 anyway.

**Verdict.** This was a real reasoning miss, not bad luck. The newest likely-voter polls (Suffolk 23% and UNH 25%, two-way) bracketed the result.

## Suggestions

- **No prompt change from one question.** The election questions are now resolved, so a change aimed at ballot measures would not help this season. Any change still needs a backtest.
- **Watch for the pattern.** It is research summaries that average unlike sources (registered and likely voters, or different methods), and forecasters adjusting away from direct polls with base-rate arguments. If it shows up again, the fix to backtest would be in the research prompt: report the most relevant subset (likely voters, latest polls) separately, not one blended average.
